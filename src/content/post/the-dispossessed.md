*"You can’t crush ideas by suppressing them. You can only crush them by ignoring them. By refusing to think, refusing to change."*
*— Ursula K. Le Guin, The Dispossessed*

In 2024 I decided to read nothing but science fiction for a while. Somewhere down that rabbit hole I came across The Dispossessed by Ursula K. Le Guin. It turns up on every list of the greatest science fiction novels ever written, which was most of the convincing I needed. Neither the SF Masterworks blurb nor the Amazon listing told me much about what was actually inside it, which suited me. I prefer going into books without knowing too much, and this one rewarded that.

It had me from the first page. At barely 330 pages it isn't a large novel, especially set against some of the doorstops being published now, but Le Guin builds an entire world inside that space. She has a way of creating places that feel completely unfamiliar while making the people inside them, their arguments and their pettiness and their compromises, feel like people you already know.

The story follows Shevek, a physicist from Anarres, a barren moon orbiting the wealthy planet Urras. Anarres exists because of Odo, a revolutionary on Urras whose thinking the settlers carried with them. Her conclusion was that the problem with Urras wasn't any particular government or law, it was ownership itself. Once you accept that a person can own a thing, you have accepted that a person can own another person's time, and hierarchy follows. The Anarresti have a word for the people they left behind, and it isn't capitalists. It's propertarians. Her followers didn't want a reformed Urras, they wanted out, and Urras handed them the moon to be rid of a movement large enough to threaten civil war. What they built there answers everything they left: no property, no money, no government, no laws, and a language constructed to make it awkward to say a thing is yours. There has been almost no contact since. Then a university on Urras invites Shevek to come and work, and for the first time since the settlement, somebody crosses.

People leave developing countries for places that will never entirely accept them as their own, not because the new country is better in any absolute sense but because the system at home is corrupt enough that reform has stopped looking possible. Leaving becomes the only option still on the table. The Odonians did the same thing, with the advantage of somewhere genuinely empty to go.

Odo herself never appears. She died long before the novel opens, and everything on Anarres is built on what she left behind in writing. Long after finishing the book, and after reading more of Le Guin, I found I couldn't stop picturing Odo as Le Guin herself. A revolutionary mind, permanently suspicious of authority and inherited norms. Maybe the novel was her way of testing her own thinking, working out what she would actually build if somebody handed her a world from scratch, and then being honest enough to show it going wrong in places.

Urras felt like Earth. Not literally, but in the way people behaved. Wealth, politics, ownership, hierarchy, all of it immediately recognisable. Shevek arrives and can't get his bearings, and it isn't the big things that throw him. It's money changing hands, being waited on by people paid to wait on him, the idea that an object can belong to one person and not another, women kept out of the sciences and treated as decoration. Nobody on Urras finds any of it worth a second thought.

During my posting in China I spent a weekend in Dandong, on the Chinese side of the Yalu River, close enough to see the buildings on the North Korean bank. You can stand there looking at a place run on completely different terms from your own and still have no idea what living inside it would do to you. Shevek doesn't get to stay on his side of the river. He is put down in the middle of Urras and has to work it out from there.

Urras is the obvious target, but the book is far more interested in Anarres, which is meant to be an anarchist society. There's no conventional government telling people how to live. Odo's philosophy is built on freedom, cooperation, mutual aid and resistance to authoritarian power, and on paper it sounds close to utopian. But people are people, and what Le Guin does so well is show the unwritten rules that grow in the space where laws would otherwise have been. Nobody writes them down and nobody enforces them, and people follow them anyway. The society doesn't have to tell you what to do. It only has to make you understand what happens if you don't. You can be ostracised, treated differently, quietly denied opportunities, or simply made to feel that you are being difficult for questioning something everyone else has already accepted. Shevek runs into it himself long before he ever leaves for Urras. He is working on a theory that could change what his people are capable of, and there are people on Anarres who would rather he didn't finish it.

Removing formal authority doesn't automatically make people free.

Nobody survives alone in the modern world. Society functions because everyone contributes to it and everyone depends on it. Being completely ostracised isn't being left in peace, it's being cut off from the system that makes ordinary survival possible. It would take an enormous amount of willpower to keep doing even basic things once the people around you have decided you don't belong. That makes ostracism a remarkably efficient form of control, and it requires no formal authority at all.

What it produces is groupthink. A group starts valuing harmony and agreement over any real examination of what it's doing, and arrives at decisions that almost nobody in it would defend alone. Anarres has no state enforcing anything. It doesn't need one.

That was the part that felt familiar. Growing up in India I've watched collective expectation do exactly the same work. What to study, where to live, who to marry, whether to have children, what counts as respectable, what the extended family has already decided is best for you. Nobody says you must. You just know what follows if you don't.

I've gone against a fair amount of it. I chose not to marry, not to have children, to give up religion, and to put myself first when making decisions about my own life. I'm not suggesting those are the right choices for anyone else. I just don't think an unwritten social contract should be mistaken for a law of nature.

I don't think Le Guin was holding Anarres up as the answer either. Odo may have had the right intentions, but an idea changes once generations start living inside it. Something built to prevent authority slowly develops its own conventions, its own hierarchy, its own rigidity. A society founded on questioning authority can become so attached to its founding principles that questioning them stops being acceptable.

We do the same thing with capitalism. Somewhere along the way we collectively decided it's the only system under which the world can function and prosper, that there will always be someone doing the oppressing and someone being oppressed, and that this is simply how things are. Even our imagination struggles to get out. Most of the futures we write are capitalist futures with better phones.

Odo's anarchism isn't the bomb-in-the-coat-pocket kind, and it isn't the economic libertarianism of the far right either. It's built on cooperation, solidarity and mutual aid, with the authoritarian state as the thing it stands against. The book never reads as an argument for tearing society down, but for refusing to inherit anything without examining it first.

I'm not an anarchist. But I have enough spine to push back when a practice is outdated and needs changing, and that might be closer to what Le Guin was getting at. A utopia can't be a fixed place where somebody finally works out the correct way for humans to live, because there is no universal human being. You can design the most intelligent system imaginable and someone won't fit inside it. Someone will find the loophole, or resist it, or decide that the thing everyone else considers normal is absurd. A better society has to stay capable of questioning itself and of changing when it needs to. Change is the only constant, which every society seems to know and then forget, going rigid instead. That rigidity is usually what brings it down. Octavia Butler built an entire religion on the same idea in [Parable of the Sower](/blog/parable-of-the-sower).

Shevek goes to Urras and comes back without a verdict. Neither world turns out to be the good one. Both were built by people and both are flawed in the ways people are flawed. And unlike the Odonians who left Urras for good, what Shevek wants by the end is to go home and argue with his own society rather than walk away from it. Which is the opposite of what Odo did. She left and built from nothing, and generations later her moon had developed an orthodoxy of its own. Building from scratch again would only mean discovering a new set of flaws in another hundred years.

The questions in this book will get into your head. You will find yourself questioning society, people, the whole idea of the collective, at odd hours and in the middle of unrelated conversations. I ended up talking about it with friends over drinks, none of whom read much and none of whom have any particular interest in science fiction. The one it left me with is how much of my own life was shaped by rules nobody ever bothered to write down.
