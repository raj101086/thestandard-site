These days the only travel that excites me is on two wheels. So when Josh, my manager, told me at the end of August to plan for a week at our office in Newcastle in November, the first thing that came to mind wasn't kangaroos or the Opera House. It was the visa formalities, then hopping flights and wrecking my sleep schedule to land in a foreign country, and all that effort for one week.

Everyone apart from me was excited. My parents told anyone who would listen that I was going to Australia. I never understood what the big deal was, but for an older generation of Indians, foreign travel is still a big deal. Nervous I wasn't. As long as my papers are in order, no country worries me, and after the [cold welcome I got in Russia](/blog/the-russian-welcome), anywhere else is a cakewalk. The only real decision was the camera. I was in two minds about taking the [X-T30](/blog/fujifilm-x-t30) or finally pulling the trigger on something new, an X-E5 or an X100VI, but the Diwali offers on Amazon didn't throw up a good deal, so I packed the X-T30 with the 55-200, and my iPhone 17 Pro.

The two times I'd met Josh this year, he was the one who had come a long way. He'd flown in to Coimbatore, and I'd flown down from Pune to meet him there. In November it was my turn, my first time in Australia and the farthest south I've ever been.

The days leading up to the trip had been hectic, with an impulsive ride home to Durg for Diwali and a return journey that got derailed by a cut tyre. I wrote [about all of it](/blog/pune-to-durg) in the Mumbai lounge while I waited for my flight. After Mumbai to Colombo and Colombo to Sydney, I landed at about 5 on a Saturday evening and walked through customs without anyone asking me a single question. There was a biometric scan and a slip on which I declared a few things, and based on that I was sent to a line where my bags went through a scanner. No officer spoke to me and nobody looked at a document, and the whole thing took less than 15 minutes. I was expecting the usual questions when I landed back in India, and compared to that, this was just ridiculous. As it turned out, coming home through Mumbai didn't take long either. Maybe things have become more streamlined since I last travelled. Like the visa, which I'd applied for online and which came through in four weeks, the tedious process I'd dreaded turned out to be mostly in my head.

Josh was flying in from Albany, and the plan was for him to drive us up. So for the first hour in his country, the guest waited for the host. His flight was on time but his bags weren't, because the conveyor belt had broken down. When I told him how smooth their customs had been, he said that unless you're bringing in something that could harm the country's biodiversity, you're in no trouble. Once they came out we picked up a rented Mazda and set off at around 7.

He'd already found us a place for dinner, a Thai restaurant about half an hour out of Sydney on the way. I'd skipped breakfast on the flight and had nothing but a black coffee, so I was starving, and the food was fantastic: scallops, rice and duck in red curry.

![Scallops at a Thai restaurant on the road from Sydney to Newcastle, my first meal in Australia](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657947/IMG_5227_jvzi3b.jpg)

We reached the Gateway Inn in Newcastle at about 10:30. By then I'd been travelling for close to 24 hours, since leaving Pune by road for the evening flight out of Mumbai, and I was knackered. Before I'd left, Josh had given me a plan for the jet lag: don't sleep on the flight from Mumbai to Colombo, don't sleep on the drive to Newcastle, and sleep only five or six hours on the eleven-hour flight from Colombo to Sydney. I followed it to the T, and when I woke up on Sunday morning, there was no jet lag at all.

The plan for Sunday was breakfast and coffee at a café. We were already in the car in the hotel parking when he asked what I wanted to do after that. I asked whether we'd be coming back to the hotel first. He said no, we'd go on directly, so I asked him to wait a minute and ran back up to the room for my camera.

We drove towards the beach, and in daylight I got my first proper look at Newcastle. It's the second-largest city in New South Wales after Sydney, about 160 kilometres to the north, built where the Hunter River meets the Pacific. On one side is the biggest coal-exporting harbour in the world, and on the other a run of beaches, with the city rising and falling on the hills between them. On the way we stopped at a beautiful café on the Honeysuckle waterfront, facing the water. I ordered an iced Americano, and Josh told me that here it's called a long black, which is worth knowing for someone who drinks as much [black coffee](/blog/black-coffee) as I do. It was the first of a week's worth of questions from me, and he answered every one of them. I had the pulled pork Benedict, and it was lip-smacking.

![Pulled pork Benedict at a café on the Honeysuckle waterfront, Sunday morning](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657947/IMG_5233_n3foxa.jpg)

After breakfast he checked the weather app, which said it wouldn't rain for a few hours, so we went to the beach first. We drove slowly along the coast road above the Pacific until we found a decent parking spot. It was chilly and windy under an overcast sky, not a perfect day for the beach, but I liked it. We walked a couple of kilometres along Nobbys Beach on the path shared by cyclists and walkers, and near the surf club I saw people surfing for the first time in my life. I took a few frames with the 55-200, and a few of them came out sharp.

![A surfer on a wave at Nobbys Beach, Newcastle](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795582953/DSCF9844_alitzv.jpg)


After an hour and a half we walked back to the car, which was a decent effort for someone like me who doesn't exercise. Newcastle is a coastal town, the shared path is pretty undulating, and the way back turned into a proper incline. Even in that chilly weather, I was a bit sweaty by the time we got there.

At the car, Josh asked me what I wanted to do next. The choice was Oakvale Wildlife Park or a winery for a wine tasting. I chose Oakvale, partly because I knew Josh wouldn't be able to drink while he was driving, and partly because I was more excited to see lazy koalas and hopping kangaroos. Oakvale keeps a few of the animals native to Australia: kangaroos, koalas, Tasmanian devils, emus and plenty of birds, along with an ostrich, for some reason. It's small but well maintained. We bought feed at the ticket counter, and I fed the kangaroos from my palm. I was a bit scared at first, but they were friendly and chill, lying around or hopping here and there with no fear of us at all. The same feed went to the ducks, the birds and a camel. We even held some out to the koala from a distance, but he was happy with his eucalyptus.

![The ostrich at Oakvale Wildlife Park](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795601953/DSCF9890_hohgmv.jpg)

The camel was a surprise. A bigger one came when Josh told me that Australia has more wild camels than anywhere else in the world. They were brought over in the 19th century to carry goods across the outback, many of them with handlers from British India and Afghanistan, and when trucks and railways took over, they were let loose and bred rapidly in the dry interior.

After the park we stopped for lunch at a roadside café run by a few Indians. Josh had a burger and I ordered a sandwich, and it was bad. He saw the face I made after the first bite and told me that at roadside places like this, you only order something fried. I said I'd expected it to be grilled. If you want a grilled sandwich, he said, you ask for a toastie.

For dinner we went to the Newcastle Cruising Yacht Club, on the same waterfront where we'd started the day. I had fish and chips, and Josh ordered the beer after asking me what I liked. I said stout, and if they didn't have one, anything would do. He came back with a Tooheys Old, a dark ale that's about as close to a stout as most Australian taps get. He'd asked what I liked and found the nearest thing to it, and it turned out to be my favourite beer of the trip.

![My first Tooheys Old, at the Newcastle Cruising Yacht Club](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657949/IMG_5263_eb0szv.jpg)

After that first weekend, the week settled into a routine. Most mornings I had breakfast at Wests, the club across the road from the hotel, where meals went on the room bill. Josh drove us to the office, and the work, which stays out of this, took up the day. In the evenings he'd ask what I felt like doing.

Monday afternoon brought thunderstorms, but by the time we left the office the sky had cleared a little, and Josh asked if I wanted to go to the beach. He was going for a run, and I could stroll or sit somewhere, which suited me fine. We parked about a kilometre before Nobbys, he ran off ahead, and I walked the same path at my sloth-like pace. It was around 15 degrees and I hadn't packed anything warm, because in my head it was summer. I made it as far as the spot where we'd watched the surfers the day before, turned around, and Josh caught up with me on the way back. Dinner was a steak at Iron Cast Grill, back at Wests.

![The coast near Nobbys on a chilly Monday evening, around 15 degrees](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795601950/IMG_5286_zeg2k3.jpg)

On Tuesday, before we left the office, we asked a colleague if she'd like to join us for dinner. "Sorry," she said, "tonight I have a date with Robbie." We said OK, and she could tell we hadn't caught her drift. "Robbie Williams," she said. Then she told us the Foo Fighters were on Thursday. Josh and I were both excited, and as soon as she left I looked for tickets, only to find that the Foo Fighters had played Newcastle the Thursday before. We'd misheard her, and our excitement died right there.

By then I'd also learned that Josh eats dinner at 5:30. He'd wanted to go straight from the office at 5, and I told him I already have an early dinner by Indian standards and I couldn't do 5:30. Whenever he's in India, he waits eagerly for the hotel restaurant to open at 7 in the evening, and now I knew why. His body clock is set to a 5:30 dinner, and most of us in India don't eat until 8 or 8:30. It's one of many things about us he doesn't understand, along with why we start our mornings so late and why our kids aren't in bed by 7:30. So by the time we sat down at Nagisa, a Japanese place on the Honeysuckle waterfront, we were both famished. Edamame, pork belly kakuni, salmon carpaccio and gyoza weren't enough, so we added karaage, and I had an Orion Dark Lager from Okinawa, the closest thing to a stout on their tap.

![Orion Dark Lager from Okinawa, on tap at Nagisa, Honeysuckle](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657944/IMG_5309_nh9fea.jpg)

Later in the week, one of our senior people asked me how things were going and how I was liking Australia. I told him that Josh had been a great host. Those first few days were a big part of why.
