Finding good books is difficult. Finding books that leave an impression and challenge your thinking is harder still. I am always looking for books that do the latter, ones that make me question the world rather than simply pass through it.

I found Parable of the Sower through a subreddit about books. I read only that it was dystopian and set in the near future, closed the page, and ordered it. I rarely read reviews or premises before buying because I don't want someone else's expectations following me into a story. I want the book to surprise me on its own terms.

It arrived and sat on my shelf for a while. I was in a reading slump and was slow to start. One weekend I picked it up, and by Sunday evening I couldn't put it down.

The novel was published in 1993. Butler set it in 2024 and 2025.

The scene that hooked me early is quiet and devastating. Lauren's father takes a group from the neighbourhood out for shooting practice. While they're out, he goes to investigate something and comes back looking grim, tells everyone to go home. Lauren only learns later what he found, a mother and her two young children, half-eaten by feral dogs. He didn't tell anyone in the moment. Just said it was time to leave. Butler writes like that throughout the book. Blunt, unsparing, nothing softened to make it easier to swallow. It will hit you hard.

Butler's world doesn't arrive through catastrophe. It arrives through accumulated compromise. Small failures of governance, rising inequality, corporations quietly replacing public institutions, communities retreating behind walls. Nothing happens overnight. The world simply keeps making small concessions until one day the one everyone knew no longer exists.

I was reading this in mid-2025, at a point where I had started limiting my intake of Indian political content on Instagram and Reddit, the constant propaganda and religious polarisation had started taking a toll on my mental health. I thought cutting it out would help. What I hadn't anticipated was that the algorithm simply replaced my Indian feed with American content. Same division, same religious nationalism, same corrupt machinery, just more polished, better packaged, harder to see for what it was at first.

Reading Lauren's world alongside all of that made something click. The playbook is the same everywhere. Nationalist and conservative leaders across different countries, different cultures, different decades, are all running variations of the same strategy, divide along religious or ethnic lines, concentrate power, weaken institutions, manufacture enemies. The difference between now and the 1930s and 40s is not the intent. It's the packaging. Mussolini and Hitler were blunt about it. Their successors learned to be subtler. Octavia Butler had published this as a warning in 1993. Nobody paid attention then. Nobody is paying attention now.

At the centre of the novel is Lauren Olamina, who carries a condition called hyperempathy — a result of her mother's drug use during pregnancy, it causes her to physically experience other people's pain and pleasure as if it were her own. It shapes who Lauren becomes without ever becoming the dominant plot device. Butler introduces it as a subtle trait rather than a superpower, which is the right call. The novel's real subject is much larger than any single ability.

Lauren's answer to all of it is Earthseed, a philosophy, or perhaps a religion, built around the idea that God is Change. I am not religious. I'm an atheist. But I found myself drawn to that idea, not as a belief but as a way of understanding the world.

*All that you touch, you change. All that you change, changes you. The only lasting truth is change.*

I am not someone who embraces change easily. My first instinct when things shift is usually resistance. But I adapt, eventually. And I think that willingness to sit with an idea, examine it critically before accepting or rejecting it, is something more people need. Too many decisions get made on emotion alone, or because a religious text demands it, or because science says so, or because a charismatic leader says it with enough conviction, without any real thinking or dialogue happening in between. Lauren's world is what you get when enough people stop questioning.

What also stayed with me was Butler's insistence that survival is not an individual achievement. Even in a world where trust has become dangerous, Lauren keeps building communities instead of retreating behind walls. She chooses cooperation knowing full well that people can betray her.

I have become more pessimistic over the years, slowly started to believe that we have gone so far in the wrong direction that catastrophe is unavoidable. But I am trying to push back against that. Scientific discoveries keep coming. Some religious institutions are becoming more welcoming. The data says the last hundred years have been the most peaceful in human history. Maybe the answer is to look for those things deliberately, the way Lauren does, keep building, keep trusting, keep extending a hand to the next person on the road, even when it's risky.

*The world is full of painful stories. Sometimes it seems as though there aren't any other kind and yet I found myself thinking how beautiful that glint of water was through the trees.*

I've read enough science fiction to know what a world driven entirely by technology looks like, and what a world driven entirely by faith or ideology looks like. Both ends of that spectrum are dystopian. Somewhere in the middle is the difficult balance we keep failing to find, science, reason, belief and individual freedom coexisting without any one of them crushing the others. 

The best science fiction doesn't imagine impossible technologies. It imagines possible people, possible societies, and how they operate under pressure. 1984, Fahrenheit 451, Brave New World — the ones that last are the ones that understand human nature well enough to extrapolate it. Parable of the Sower belongs in that company.

There is a second book, Parable of the Talents. I read it after and found it good, a 4 out of 5. But this one is a 5. Start here and let the sequel follow naturally.
