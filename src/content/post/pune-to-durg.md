At 3 in the morning on 12 November I was behind the wheel of a small Mahindra pickup on a state highway somewhere short of the Solapur highway, with [Snowpiercer](/blog/snowpiercer-two-years-triumph-street-triple-r) tied down in the back, and no matter how hard I pressed the pedal, the van went nowhere. A few days earlier I'd been sure that riding home on an impulse was the right decision. How I thought of it at 3am was something different.

My other plans for Diwali had fallen apart, and over dinner with my girlfriend I decided to ride home to Durg instead. The next morning, 7 November, I was on my way. I left at 4:20, and I was surprised to see so many vehicles on the road to Nagar at that hour, a lot of riders among them, maybe because of the Diwali holidays. It was rush and stop and go until Nagar, and in places all the way to Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar. Once I was past all that, I was on the state highways of Maharashtra, which I've really come to love for two reasons. They're decently well maintained, and there are hardly any cattle or stray dogs to come in the way, so I can hold high three-digit speeds even there. I'd lost almost an hour in the traffic, so I went berserk after that, and at 6:30 in the evening I was home. I'm a humble person, but if there's one thing I can brag about, it's being punctual. This was the third time I've ridden home, and all three times I've reached within 30 minutes of 6:30, which over 1,000 kilometres on a motorcycle is still awesome. Since I set that time, I've had a kind of mental clock that gets me there by then.

On Diwali in 2024 I'd surprised my mother in the kitchen after riding home on Snowpiercer, but she'd known I was coming home then. She just hadn't known I'd be riding. This time I'd had no plans of going home at all, so she wasn't expecting me, and there I was, standing in the kitchen in my riding gear, asking her what she was cooking for the next day's pooja. She was overjoyed, and she immediately shouted to my father that I'd come. It wasn't the first time I'd realised that festivals are meant to be celebrated with family and the people you love, but at that moment the impulsive decision I'd made over dinner the night before felt worth it.

The festival went well. I met my friends, spent time with the family and ate all the scrumptious festival snacks my mother had made. I also met an old school friend, and went to see my college for the first time in 19 years since passing out.

I left for Pune on the morning of 11 November at 4:15. The evening before, I'd filled up with petrol, checked the tyre pressures and lubed the chain. It was a bit chilly, and the [Braddan mesh jacket](/blog/riding-gear) was just about enough to keep me warm. Any colder and I'd have felt it in my bones. On the way to Durg I'd already found that the headlight was pretty weak for highway riding, so I started off slow. Just after Dongargarh there's a dense patch of forest, and I rode through it at a steady 80 to 90 km/h. Within two hours I'd covered almost 180 kilometres, and at first light I broke into triple digits and let the bike flow, and off the mile markers went, one by one.

Soon I was standing outside the Nagpur bypass, checking maps before taking the highway towards Yavatmal and Nanded and on to Pune. It was a new route for me, and I'd chosen it for one reason: it avoided the Nagar traffic, which I'd dreaded since seeing the state of it a few days earlier. The last time I rode back from Durg, in February this year, that stretch had cost me almost two hours. The new road was in good condition and I was flying most of the way. By 10:30 I'd done roughly 498 kilometres in six hours. At that pace I could already see myself in Pune by 4, maybe even going out for dinner with my girlfriend, who was on her way back from Goa.

Maybe I was gloating a bit too much inside the helmet, because the universe decided to take my happiness down a few notches. I was lost in those thoughts when a loud bang and a little swerve of the motorcycle broke the trance. I knew straight away it was a flat.

It was the start of a ghat, so I stopped, turned around and walked the bike a few metres back towards a dhaba with a tyre repair shop next to it. A couple of motorcyclists stopped to ask what had happened, and when I said I had a flat, they told me there was a shop just ahead. I was still in high spirits. A flat can be fixed, I thought, and I'd be back on the road in a few minutes. Boy, was I in for a surprise. Even dragging the bike to the shop took a massive effort, and by the time I got there I was short of breath. The repairman asked me to put it on the main stand, and when I said there wasn't one, I offered to roll it back and forth until he found the puncture. He found it immediately. I was shocked by this brilliance, because usually they have to throw water on the tyre to find the hole, but when the hole is as big as the one in my rear tyre, no further investigation is needed. There was a massive cut right in the centre of the tread. He said there was no way to fix it as it was, and gave me two options. One was to change the tyre, which was obvious. The other was to take the tyre off, patch it inside and out, and put a tube in it. I said I'd rather be towed than take the risk of a tube in a damaged tubeless tyre. The speeds are way too high, and it wasn't worth the risk. At 10:40 I messaged my roadside assistance (RSA) provider to ask for a tow.

![The cut in the centre of Snowpiercer's rear tyre, too big for a puncture repair](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1794735174/IMG_5135_umnxph.jpg)

The dhaba was a Punjabi one on NH 361, just past the Bijora toll plaza. Next to the puncture shop there were khats laid out for truck drivers. I piled all my riding gear on one, sat down next to it and spent the afternoon scrolling, texting and following up with the RSA. I told everyone close to me what had happened, and my parents got worried, which I don't like. It's just not in my nature to make others worry about me, and it frustrates me, and sometimes I even get angry with them when they're only showing genuine concern.

The RSA sent me the driver's details at 12:10, and I shared my location with him. Then I waited, and waited, and grew impatient, because I couldn't understand why it was taking so long. Every time I called, he said it would take at least an hour and a half. When I called an hour and a half later, he said 45 minutes more, so I had lunch at the dhaba: dal tadka, bhindi fry and roti. I think that bhindi was the only good thing to happen all day. When I called after 45 minutes and he said 30, I lost it and asked him why he wasn't driving faster. He didn't say anything. I only understood why at 3 the next morning.

The six hours weren't wasted. They were full of curious people asking about the bike and the ride, where I'd come from and where I was going. Others were concerned and wanted to help, with suggestions about how I could fix the tyre and where I might find a new one. I listened to them all politely, even when I knew their ideas weren't going to work, because the kind-heartedness behind them was genuine. Every time I talk to strangers on a ride I feel there's hope for humanity, and then I go online and see nothing but vitriol and negativity. Maybe I should ride more and come online less.

As soon as I saw the van come round the bend, I knew it was going to be a long ride. It was a small Mahindra pickup, and the driver had music playing at a blazing volume, Emraan Hashmi songs. I waved from across the road, he turned a little further ahead and stopped next to the bike, and the driver, a couple of guys from the dhaba and I lifted Snowpiercer onto the back and tied her down. I left my jacket and helmet in the back next to her and kept my backpack, my only luggage, in front with me, because it had my laptop in it. The cabin was just big enough for two, and a big wooden woofer had been fitted in it, so I couldn't stretch my legs. We left at 3:40.

![Snowpiercer tied down on the back of the Mahindra pickup](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1794735178/IMG_5160_bzoiu3.jpg)

Within five minutes, after a quick mental calculation, I knew how long this was going to take. At first the ETA on Google Maps said 2:30am, and it kept creeping up as our pace dropped. The driver was a jovial, casual guy in no hurry at all, and he talked on the phone the whole two and a half hours to Nanded. The loud music and the endless talking frustrated me and made me angry, but I didn't say anything.

After Nanded we turned towards a place called Kandhar. I took a photo of the sign, because I found it funny that Maharashtra has a Kandhar of its own. The Kandahar most of us know is the one in Afghanistan, where the hijackers took Indian Airlines flight IC 814 in 1999. Kandhar turned out to be the driver's hometown, and he told me he'd been there at 12:10 when he got my location. I felt slightly guilty for judging him for driving slowly or being late, because I only realised how far that was once I'd travelled the same road with him in the van.

![Kandhar, the driver's hometown in Maharashtra](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1794735172/IMG_5170_iiniex.jpg)

He stopped at a junction in the middle of the market to pick up someone to keep him company on the drive back from Pune. I got down to stretch my legs and asked how long we'd have to wait. Twenty minutes, he said. I was frustrated again, but this time I didn't want to shout or lose my patience, because I didn't want the universe to have one over me. So I stood there scrolling until I noticed people stopping to look at the bike. Some started talking to the driver, and by then he'd got enough out of me to tell them all about it. Then some young guys came over with the usual questions: which bike is it, how much did it cost, what's the top speed, is it fun to ride. I had nothing better to do, so I answered them all with enthusiasm. One of them was a bit of a blabbermouth, and he got so excited that he took my number and said he'd come to Pune so I could take him to the showroom for a test ride. This went on for 25 minutes before I finally told the driver we should leave. He agreed reluctantly, and we left without his companion.

It was 7:30 when we left Kandhar, and the ETA was now 4:20am. The driver had a new grit and determination about him. Maybe he was angry that his friend had ditched him, and it showed in his driving. He floored the accelerator, and we cruised at our top speed of 65 km/h for the next few hours. He was so determined that he didn't want to stop for tea, food or water. After four hours of driving without a break, I asked him to stop at a tea stall, and we had water and tea and carried on. We went by way of Ahmedpur, avoiding the main highways, on state highways most of the way. Our last tea stop was at about 2:30.

Around 3 he stopped to take a leak and then asked me to drive. He didn't even ask whether I knew how. He just assumed I did. I took pity on him and got behind the wheel, and I tried my level best to keep up a decent pace and failed miserably. I couldn't hold 65 consistently, though at one point I did see 66 on the dash, unless I was hallucinating by then. The van, he told me, had been speed-locked by the company because the frame wasn't sturdy enough to survive an impact at high speed. That was the moment I understood his silence on the phone, when I'd asked him why he wasn't driving faster. After about 30 kilometres, when we reached the Solapur highway, I gave him back the wheel. I felt he'd drive faster and more safely on the highway than I could, steering that van for the first time.

We reached the entrance of my society at 4:30 in the morning. I'd been awake for 25 hours, and it was just over 24 hours since I'd left home in Durg. It took us ten minutes to unload, and I parked Snowpiercer in the parking and put the covers on her. I gave the driver a ₹500 tip, thanked him for his monstrous effort and wished him a safe drive back.

I'm writing this in the airport lounge a day later, waiting for my flight to Sydney. Snowpiercer is still under her covers with a cut rear tyre, and I'm looking for new rubber, so that I'll have something lined up by the time I'm back. Sitting here now, I'd rate the trip a 6 out of 10. Would I do it again? Hell yes!
