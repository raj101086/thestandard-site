On 14 February 2003 I lost [my grandmother](/blog/sambar) to sickness and old age. With all the commotion at home I couldn't study well enough, and I barely scraped through class 11. I was sure I'd fail chemistry, and I only just passed.

My father's answer was a crash course, coaching for the 12th boards and the competitive exams that came after them. The classes were rough. During school days they ran every evening from 5 to 8, and once the summer vacation started they ran from 2 to 8. For the first month the coaching centre was in Sector 9, and when the hours got longer it moved to Civic Center. I pedalled to all of it. On school days that meant cycling twice, once in the morning from home to school in Sector 10 and back, and then out again in the evening for coaching. This went on for four months, and by the end of it I'd had enough.

I'd already asked for a motorcycle, and my parents had said no, citing finances. Meanwhile [Abhinav](/blog/the-cd-player), my friend from across the road, had got his, a Hero Honda Passion. As usual he had it first, and it made me even more frustrated and even more determined.

I'd also learned to ride by then. During the vacation I used to go to a local video game shop to play on the PS2, and the guy who ran it, Veere, taught Abhinav and me to ride on his Splendor. One day, frustrated with my begging for a motorcycle, my father asked me to show him whether I could actually ride. A family friend had come over on his Splendor, so I sat on it and kick-started it, the bhaiya sat behind me, and I let the clutch go. The front wheel popped up. My father turned around and went back inside.

Sometime in late June, I came out of class at 8:30, after it had run late, and found that my cycle had been stolen. I called my mother from the coaching office, she called my father's cousin in Sector 10, and he came and dropped me home. My mother's first thought was that I'd sold the cycle myself so that I'd get the motorcycle. I'm not the devious schemer she took me for.

I still don't know what the trigger was, but soon after that my father finally decided to get me the motorcycle. I wanted a Hero Honda Passion, just like Abhinav's, but my father had other ideas, and he decided on the Bajaj Caliber 115, the Hoodibaba, the bike behind those strange ads that ran all through the 2003 World Cup before anyone knew what they were for. He went to book it with the same bhaiya whose Splendor I'd popped a wheelie on, probably because the bhaiya rode a motorcycle and my father only rode a Chetak.

My father believed in numerology, so the registration number had to add up to 7. The Caliber's was 1231. He kept it up after that, and most of our cars and motorcycles since have had numbers that add up to 7. The Punto was 7207. Even [Snowpiercer](/blog/snowpiercer-two-years-triumph-street-triple-r) came out as 9817, purely by accident.

On the afternoon of 10 July 2003, my father, my brother, the bhaiya and I went to collect it. It was black, with green stripes all over it: on the front, the tank, the side panels and the rear panels. We did the paperwork, left the showroom and went straight to the Hanuman Mandir in Sector 9 for the pooja, and got home around 6.

Once I had it, I rode that bike everywhere. I rode it to school and coaching, and later to college, every day, and in the evenings to Supela or [Civic Center](/blog/civic-center-food-trucks) for a bite to eat, or to Sector 10 to fetch a CD of a new film. The long rides were usually to Dongargarh, twice a year during Navratri, for the Bamleshwari temple, and to villages nearby where friends had relatives. Back then 100 or 200 kilometres felt massive. The roads weren't made for long rides, and I didn't have the money to go much further anyway. It also carried us to numerous cricket tournaments, three and sometimes four of us sitting on it at once. Looking back now, we were just dumb, with total disregard for safety and risk.

There were a number of accidents too, each one freakier than the last. The one I remember best was our own version of the infamous scene in Kaho Naa... Pyaar Hai where Hrithik's motorcycle skids and he slides off a bridge, minus the bridge. My friend Tushaar, who's no longer with us, loved speed as much as I did. One day we were flying along when a milkman, with milk cans hanging on both sides of his cycle, came from the front and took an immediate left. I braked, but the drum brakes weren't enough to stop in time. We slammed into his cycle and skidded along the road with it, and every can of milk spilled. There I was, picking up my motorcycle and checking it for damage, and there was Tushaar, having a fight with the milkman.

I rode the Caliber for seven years, from 2003 until 2010, when I left Bhilai for Jamshedpur. I finished my 12th on it. My mother still has a framed photo of me sitting on it, ready to leave for my first day of college. Then came my engineering, and after that I rode it to my first job every morning. Over those years I did [all sorts of modifications](/blog/cost-of-ownership) to it, from low-riser handlebars to lights around the rear wheel that lit up whenever I braked. Funnily enough, the two motorcycles I own now are both completely stock. After me, my brother did his engineering on it too. It worked fine until the end. When he moved to Bangalore he took it with him, used it for a year or so and finally sold it for next to nothing. By then it had close to 150,000 kilometres on it.

It took me through everything: good times and bad times, broke days, heartbreak, all those rides and all those accidents, and it rode on through all of it and did its job. My brother and I have both come a long way since the days of riding the Hoodibaba through the lanes of Bhilai, but we'll always have a soft corner for it. It was the first bike that was ours. I still feel good whenever I see one on the road, and yes, you can still see them. That's how good that engine was.
