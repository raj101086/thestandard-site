They say never meet your heroes. My Royal Enfield Interceptor is the proof.

My brother and I grew up without a car or a motorcycle. The only thing in the garage was an old yellow Chetak that my dad rode everywhere. I think that's exactly why we both ended up the way we did, noticing every bike on the road, having opinions about all of them.

My earliest motorcycle memory isn't even a real motorcycle. It's Street Hawk, the show that used to air on Doordarshan in the early 90s, about a crime-fighting hero on a black sportsbike. I watched it with my dad. It was the coolest thing I'd ever seen, and I still think so.

My first real bike was a [Bajaj Caliber 115, the Hoodibaba](/blog/hoodibaba), launched during the 2003 World Cup, the one India lost in the final. That bike did close to 150,000 kilometres between the two of us. I rode it through college, then my brother did the same.

In 2013 my brother and I came close to buying our first big bike. We put down a deposit of ₹5,000 on a Royal Enfield Desert Storm 500, and then we had to let it go, because my father needed to replace his Chetak. Instead of the Desert Storm, I bought him a Honda Aviator. After that I became a car guy. Once the [Punto](/blog/cost-of-ownership) arrived, I didn't ride actively for six or seven years, and the itch never really went away.

In September 2020 I got back from Rwanda, where a one-month work trip had turned into almost eight months because of the pandemic. The one good thing about being stuck there was that I'd saved some money, and it paid for a few things I'd had my eye on for a while. First came the [Tissot Seastar](/blog/every-watch-ive-owned), then the [Fuji camera](/blog/fujifilm-x-t30), and then my brother and I finally started planning the bike.

There wasn't much discussion about which one. It had to be the Interceptor, the motorcycle we'd talked about from the day it launched in 2018, back when owning one felt like a distant thing. We went for Baker Express, which we both still think is the best colour scheme for a bike of this style. I was in Pune, so my brother took the delivery in Bhilai on 21 May 2021, and it's still registered in his name.

I reached Bhilai five days later, on 26 May, and rode it for the first time that evening. I still get goosebumps remembering it: the first proper twist of the throttle and the engine roaring down Central Avenue. It was a pure rush of adrenaline.

My friend had asked what I'd name the bike. I thought about it and landed on Mashooqa. She's that beautiful, the kind of motorcycle you find yourself staring at in the garage for no reason. What I didn't know then was how fitting the name would turn out to be.

In mid-June, once the Aspida helmet and the DSG jacket I'd ordered online had arrived, I rode her to Pune. I left home at 5 in the morning and went straight to the Hanuman Mandir in Sector 9, where the Caliber had had its pooja eighteen years earlier, and from there I set off west. Somewhere along the way I realised I wasn't going to make Pune that day, so I asked my brother to book me a room. By the time I got near Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar it was raining heavily. I reached the Lemon Tree there around 8, completely drenched, took a hot shower and fell into bed without dinner. The next morning I rode the rest of the way and reached Pune around 2 in the afternoon.

From then until July 2024, when I bought [Snowpiercer](/blog/snowpiercer-two-years-triumph-street-triple-r), I rode Mashooqa often. Every Saturday was a long ride, towards Solapur, [Tamhini](/blog/tamhini_1), Mahabaleshwar, Nashik or Lonavala. Somewhere in those years she developed a problem the Royal Enfield service centre couldn't pin down, let alone fix. She ran rough every time I let go of the throttle.

Then Snowpiercer arrived, and from 2024 until the middle of 2025 I barely started Mashooqa. She spent most of that time under a cover, started only now and then, and came out properly only when the Triumph went in for repairs. With an imported sportsbike that can mean fifteen or twenty days at the service centre, and that's when a second bike comes in handy. That rough running was finally sorted in 2025 by Pitlane, an independent garage, and it turned out to be the tappets, which only needed adjusting. Since then, touch wood, she's been riding like new, and I take her out once a week again. My girlfriend prefers her too. She's far more comfortable on the Interceptor, and far less terrified for her life, than she is on Snowpiercer.

Five years in, the honest verdict: ride quality is average, RE's service is pathetic, the parts are middling, and the stock tyres are wrong for a bike this heavy. As for the brakes, in a genuine emergency, prayer is about as effective. That combination of mediocre tyres and uninspiring brakes is not something you want to discover at speed.

And yet I still have her. None of this is easy to forgive, but she was a long time coming, and she belongs to a particular time in our lives. Selling her would feel like selling that too.

So I'm torn. I can keep her for what she means, or accept that some love affairs aren't built to last. I still don't know which it'll be.
