In 2023 I got bored of the mainstream streaming platforms. Prime, Netflix and Disney had started to feel like the same show in different packaging, and I wanted something different. That's where MUBI came in. The subscription was cheap, I think ₹149 for three months, and it turned out to be worth every rupee.

I already knew what I wanted to watch first. A photography channel on YouTube had been breaking down some of the night shots from Chungking Express, his 1994 film, and the stills alone were enough. That was my introduction to Wong Kar-wai. Then came In the Mood for Love from 2000, and after that 2046 and Fallen Angels.

His films aren't for everyone. They're not in English, the stories feel fragmented, and sometimes nothing much happens at all. There are long silences, lingering shots, conversations left unfinished and emotions left unspoken. If you want a tight plot, you'll spend most of the film wondering what the fuss is about, and then you'll rewind a scene for the third time because you can't believe how beautiful it looks.

If I had to describe his films to someone, I'd compare them to those videos of painters who seem to throw paint at the canvas at random. At first you think, what is this, anyone could do that. Then, in front of your eyes, it starts to change. What looked abstract turns out to have been intentional all along, and before you realise it there's a masterpiece in front of you.

As someone who shoots photographs, I was pulled in by the cinematography before the story. I went down a YouTube rabbit hole trying to figure out what cameras and lenses Christopher Doyle used, and what came out of it was the range of what he and Wong could do together. In Chungking Express everything looks rushed, motion-blurred and alive, as if the film itself can't sit still. In the Mood for Love does the opposite. Every scene holds like a painting that comes to life slowly as the camera pans. It's the same director and the same cinematographer, working in two entirely different worlds.

Chungking Express is all frantic motion, neon and the crowded streets of Hong Kong. Every frame looks spontaneous, as if they caught something they weren't supposed to. And then Faye Wong dances alone in the apartment of Cop 663, Tony Leung's character, to California Dreamin', mouthing the words while she secretly reorganises his life without him knowing. She's in love with him and never says it. The song says it for her.

In the Mood for Love is a different film entirely: slow pans, narrow corridors, rain-soaked streets, and glances that do more work than any dialogue could. It's a film about two people who fall in love and do nothing about it. That restraint is the whole point, and it's devastating. Yumeji's Theme plays and replays throughout, that slow, aching waltz, and every time it comes back the film gets heavier. One of the most memorable scenes is the stairwell. Chow and Su, played by Tony Leung and Maggie Cheung, pass each other on the narrow steps, with no words and barely a glance, and the score does everything the two of them cannot.

I'd lived in China before, on a posting in [Dalian](/blog/chinese-hot-pot-my-last-meal) in 2017, and I knew that specific loneliness of being surrounded by millions of people and still being alone in a foreign land. These films brought it back. In December 2023, a few months after I first watched them, I had one free evening in [Shanghai](/photography/an-evening-in-shanghai) between a bullet train and a flight home, and Wong Kar-wai was definitely on my mind. It shows in three of the shots from that night, which I shot with a cooler tint than I normally would: the faces in the window of a bus waiting to move, a girl in a bunny hat just off the main road, and a man by the river that I shot from a distance, because I don't like being close to my subject.

But the Shanghai I walked through is so far from his Hong Kong of the 90s that the two are almost impossible to imagine side by side. The biggest difference is the pace. Hong Kong was fast even then, but nothing compares to modern Shanghai. Of everything that's changed since those films were made, it's the slowness I miss most, and I'm not alone in that. So many people are exhausted by information overload and non-stop notifications that even those born after 2000 long for a life they've never lived. It's easier for me, because I lived that life until I was about eighteen, when college and [the Caliber](/blog/hoodibaba) put the internet within easy reach. These days what I long for is a slow life of my own: a siesta every afternoon, a glass of wine when I wake up, a walk to the market for fresh oranges, and an evening in the park watching the sunset. I know all of that is possible. It just takes real effort to break out of the fast-paced digital prison we've built for ourselves.

In the Mood for Love captures a city that's mostly gone now, and watching it feels like looking through someone else's old photographs and recognising the feeling anyway. Wong Kar-wai's films are about what doesn't happen: the missed chances, the half-finished conversations, the moments you keep coming back to precisely because nothing was resolved. Start with Chungking Express, and see where it takes you.
