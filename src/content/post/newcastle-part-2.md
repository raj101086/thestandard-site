The [first half of my week in Newcastle](/blog/newcastle-part-1) belonged to Josh, who drove me around, fed me and taught me the words. The second half was about getting ready to manage without him, and on Wednesday we started preparing me for it. Josh was leaving for Sydney early on Friday, and I'd be on my own for the last two days, so that evening we took an Uber to see if it would work for me. It also meant Josh could have a drink. We ate at the Oceane on Honeysuckle, where the food was OK-ish. After a couple of beers Josh found a citrusy New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc, and over it he told me how his love of wine began, with a couple of months at a winery straight after graduation. His cellar at home now goes back to around 2007.

A few glasses in, he was energised and went looking for a hidden bar he'd been to a couple of years ago: Coal & Cedar on Hunter Street, a Prohibition-style speakeasy with no sign and no doorbell. Josh explained something about codes and a barber shop, and I was too drunk to understand any of it. Luckily the bartender was standing outside and led us straight in, so we missed the mini adventure. Inside it was old-fashioned and dimly lit, with a nice vibe. I had an Australian gin and tonic and then two pints of dark lager. I booked the Uber home at 10:30, the test passed, and we were at the hotel by 10:45. I woke up with a mild hangover, but I guess that's expected when you're 40.

![Inside Coal & Cedar, the hidden bar on Hunter Street, Newcastle](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795601952/IMG_5332_osehun.jpg)

On Thursday, as the work wound down, Greg took over as my guide. He's a colleague who grew up in Newcastle and has spent his whole life around it, and after four days of Josh's Newcastle, this was a local's. We started along Nobbys again, at a vantage point where I could see the Nobbys surf house, the Newcastle Ocean Baths and everything else on either side. He showed me a few old houses that had been shelled in 1942, when a Japanese submarine surfaced off the coast and the guns of Fort Scratchley, just above Nobbys, fired back. Greg's father and grandfather used to tell him how afraid people in Newcastle were that the Japanese would invade. He also told me about the tunnels under Fort Scratchley, which, he said, run from near the lighthouse all the way across to somewhere past King Edward Park. They run tours of them now, and he was planning to do one.

Then he told me that a storm had once dragged a huge ship onto the beach. I didn't believe him, given the sheer size of these ships, until he showed me the photos of the Pasha Bulker, a coal carrier stranded on Nobbys Beach in 2007, and I was left speechless.

From there we walked through King Edward Park. It was a hot day, there was a wedding in the gardens, and I took some photos from the cliff. While I was looking out for whales, Greg pointed down to a spot where people were swimming in a shallow pool cut into the rock. It's called the Bogey Hole. Greg said convicts had cut it as punishment; in fact, around 1820, they were made to carve it as a private bath for their commandant, James Morisset. With the waves crashing in all around, the people inside were safe in a stone pool carved two hundred years ago by some delinquents.

With all the walking up and down in the hot sun I was a bit sweaty, and seeing me, Greg asked if I'd like something cold. Did I like milkshakes? On a day like that, I said, I'd love one. In Hamilton he stopped at an old-looking shop called Jim's. Walking in felt like entering a sweet shop from an American film of the sixties or seventies, with retro machines, tall stools and an array of colourful ice creams and sweets. It's been there since 1957. I had a mango thickshake and Greg had a lime one, and we wandered the street and its shops while he told me how Australians had gone from obsessing over beer to obsessing over coffee. There was a time, he said, when the pubs closed at 6, so everyone rushed there straight from work, and once that rule went, the pubs were never as full again.

![The Clocktower Café in Hamilton, seen while walking around with a mango thickshake from Jim's](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657945/IMG_5354_cxlpja.jpg)

Back at the car, he asked if I'd like a beer overlooking a lake. Just like with the milkshake, I said yes. What better way to enjoy a sunny afternoon? It was a slow drive to Lake Macquarie in the 5 o'clock traffic, past Charlestown, where Greg lives, and then we took a left and the huge lake was in front of me, the water still, sailboats out on it, and sunset about an hour away. We drove along it for ten minutes to a big club where Greg is a member. At the door he asked if I was carrying ID, and I asked if I looked that young. Not that, he said, the club just needed it. Inside it was packed for a Thursday evening, mostly older people, with a raffle going on. Membership, Greg told me, cost $10 and got you 10% off all year. I was flummoxed at how cheap it was, until he explained that with a casino in the club, the club hardly ever loses money. It made sense later, when I found out that Australians lose more money gambling per person than anyone else in the world. Greg asked what I wanted, and I found Tooheys Old on tap, the beer Josh had ordered for me on my first Sunday. We took our glasses out to the deck and sat sipping them. It was a beautiful evening.

We left at 6:30 for The Grain Store, where we had a booth booked for 7:15. Josh joined us there, along with another colleague. It was also my first time running a tab: a slip with a number on it, which I showed the bartender every time I went up for a round. It was a chill evening, and we talked about everything apart from work. Josh found out that Greg had inherited old wines from his in-laws, bottles that now go for close to $10,000 each, which for a man with a cellar going back to 2007 was quite a discovery. The fourth man at the table had been to India too, and he and Josh compared notes on it, and when we went round the table on what each of us does with our time off, he turned out to be a certified pilot with a boat of his own who rides, like me. We ordered two tasting paddles, which let me try five of their beers, and I liked the stout the most. My Grain burger was a bit dry, but the fries were fantastic. Greg showed us old photos and videos of Newcastle people rioting after a pub was closed, which must have been the Star Hotel riot of 1979. We stayed until 10, when the waitress told us it was closing time, and we were the last ones out.

![Me at The Grain Store, Newcastle, with a tasting paddle of beer samplers](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795668952/IMG_5366_vm2rih.jpg)

As I was finishing my last glass of stout, Josh asked which beer I'd liked most on the trip. Tooheys Old, I said. He was surprised, and so was Greg, and they asked whether I hadn't liked this stout. I said I did, but I can get one like it at Toit in Pune. Tooheys Old I can't.

There was more to it than that. Newcastle used to be a steel city, like [Bhilai](/blog/hoodibaba), where I grew up, until the steelworks closed in 1999. The city has modernised since, but it has kept an old bit of itself preserved, and to me Tooheys Old was part of that. Drinking it felt like travelling back to the Newcastle that existed before all the development.

On Friday I woke up with a slight buzz from all that beer. Josh had left for Sydney early that morning, so for the first time that week I was on my own. I skipped breakfast and took an Uber to work. At lunch I went with a few colleagues to a small takeaway near the office, a typical Aussie joint, and this time I took Josh's advice and ordered the greasiest thing on the menu, an Aussie burger: a slightly warm, crisp bun, a greasy patty, a fried egg, a slice of cheese and a dash of barbecue sauce. It was the best burger I had in Australia, and well worth the $16.

For dinner I went back across the road to Wests, this time to the Texas barbecue grill, where the speciality is a rib eye slow-cooked for 18 hours. I ordered onion rings, the 10-ounce steak and one last pint of Tooheys Old, my last taste of the old Newcastle. I had my eye on the dessert menu, but by the last piece of steak there was no room left.

![The 18-hour slow-cooked rib eye at the Texas barbecue grill, my last dinner in Newcastle](https://res.cloudinary.com/dpsbm1jkq/image/upload/v1795657944/IMG_5389_wgkuww.jpg)

The next morning I had a train to catch to Sydney. On paper, it looked easy.
